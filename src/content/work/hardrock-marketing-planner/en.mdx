## Approach

The core constraint wasn't a missing feature, it was a missing structure: campaign
data had four nested levels (Plan, Jobs, Campaigns, Offering Vars), and the
existing spreadsheet-based workflow gave marketing ops no reliable way to see
how a change at one level rippled into the others. Two problems came out of
that directly: no centralized visibility into what was scheduled when, and a
hierarchy complex enough that a naive UI would just move the spreadsheet's
confusion into a database-backed tool instead of fixing it.

That reframed the brief from "design a form" to "design a system": the
calendar, the hierarchy browser, and the campaign-setup flow all had to read
from and write to the same underlying structure without the UI exposing raw
data-model complexity to the person using it.

## Decisions

**Simplifying the system structure.** The four-level hierarchy stayed in the
data model but was collapsed to three visible layers in the interface —
Offering Vars are surfaced inline where they're configured rather than as a
separate navigation level, cutting a click and a context switch out of the
most repeated task.

  src="/images/work/hardrock-marketing-planner/key-decisions.png"

/>

**Protecting usability at scale.** As the number of concurrent campaigns
grew, list and calendar views were built to stay legible rather than degrade
into a wall of rows — filtering and grouping by job were treated as core
interactions, not secondary controls, since that's what kept the tool usable
once real campaign volume hit it.

**Automating setup & efficiency.** Several fields that used to require
manual entry per campaign (recurring offer variables, default scheduling
windows) were replaced with rule-based defaults derived from the parent job,
so setting up a new campaign meant confirming values instead of typing them
from scratch.

## Technical notes

The interface was built on Ant Design as the base component library, which
kept the team from re-solving already-solved interaction patterns (tables,
date pickers, form validation) and let the design effort concentrate on the
planner's actual complexity — the hierarchy and the calendar. Components were
documented in Storybook and kept in sync with the Figma source through
Zeroheight, so the design system stayed a single source of truth for both
sides instead of drifting between what was designed and what shipped.

## Results

Real, measured numbers — not projected or invented: campaign creation time
dropped by roughly 30%, tool adoption grew about 120% in the first month
after rollout, and overall usage kept growing around 30% beyond that initial
spike as more of the marketing team moved off spreadsheets and onto the
planner.
