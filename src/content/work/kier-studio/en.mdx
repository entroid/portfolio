## Approach

The project started as a positioning problem, not a design one. Before there was a layout I had to answer three questions in writing: what the studio actually sells, who is on the other side of the screen, and what counts as a good outcome for a visit.

I worked those answers out with my business partner, with our first clients, and with an LLM as a sparring partner: I argued a position, the model came back with the objections a sceptical prospect would raise, and I rewrote. That gave us the four service lines and a much narrower target. We had been saying "companies that need a website". What we actually meant was **working small businesses whose growth outpaced their manual processes**: appointments over WhatsApp, operations in spreadsheets, a site that no longer represents them. It also settled how the page opens, which is on the friction the visitor already feels.

## The loop: from structure to deployed code

With the offer settled, the rest ran as one continuous loop and not as separate design and development phases.

**Structure before surface.** I wrote the architecture as a conversion outline, with an explicit argument for the order of the sections: state the problem, prove it can be solved, show it already has been, and only then ask.

**Content before UI.** I wrote the copy before there was a layout, so the design had real text to shape.

**UI straight in code.** I built the interface as Next.js + Tailwind components instead of mocking it up and rebuilding it later. AI did the mechanical translation from intent to markup. The design decisions — hierarchy, rhythm, density, what gets cut — stayed mine, reviewed in the browser at real widths.

**Ship, then iterate on the real thing.** With GitHub and Vercel every change was previewable at a URL and in production the same day. Iterating on the published page is faster, and it is harder to fool yourself.

## Measuring: the plan, the finding and the decision

A landing page nobody measures is a hypothesis with good typography. The site shipped with PostHog and one concrete question: **which part of the argument converts, and through which channel.**

The page offers five ways to start a conversation — hero, benefits, services, process and form — plus email, WhatsApp and Instagram. Uninstrumented, that leaves a hole: when a message arrives, nothing says which section earned it, and the visitor who leaves for WhatsApp instead of scrolling to the form never shows up at all. So every entry point fires an event carrying its origin and its channel.

/>

**From the day I installed PostHog, 8 August, through 2 September, the baseline ended up answering a different question from the one I had instrumented for.** 24 unique visitors, 47 sessions, three in four arriving direct.

<CaseStudyImage
  src="/images/work/kier-studio/posthog-analytics.webp"
  alt="PostHog, 8 August to 2 September: 24 visitors, 47 sessions, three in four direct"
/>

Search Console filled in the rest: 40 impressions, **zero clicks**, average position 12.4. The only queries with any volume are the studio's own name.

<CaseStudyImage
  src="/images/work/kier-studio/google-console.webp"
  alt="Search Console, same range: 40 impressions, zero clicks, and brand queries only"
/>

So **the bottleneck is acquisition, not the site.** At that volume any reading of the funnel produces false conclusions, and an A/B test would never reach significance. Not running one was the call. Optimising the copy would have been tidy work on the wrong problem.

The site stays as it is and the measurement cycle stays open. The problem just moved: from converting better to earning traffic with commercial intent.

## Key product decisions

**• One page, one argument:** I ruled out the multi-page studio site. With a small body of work, splitting the argument across routes adds navigation without easing the reader's load. I took the trade-off knowingly: it makes page-level analytics useless and forces interest to be measured with events.

**• External channels count as conversions:** for this audience WhatsApp is the natural first contact, not a fallback. Counting form submissions alone would have undercounted the outcome the studio actually cares about.

**• A rule for what comes next:** write the hypothesis before changing anything, change one thing at a time, and record the result even when the hypothesis fails.

## Technical notes

- **• AI-assisted at every stage:** different LLMs, coding tools and agents, with prompt and context engineering. The direction and the decisions were mine; AI accelerated the execution.

- **• Stack:** Next.js + Tailwind, deployed to Vercel from GitHub. No CMS: the content lives in the repository, which is what makes sense on a site whose copy is edited by the same person who writes the components.

- **• Analytics:** PostHog with named custom events carrying `origin` and `channel` properties. Autocapture works as a safety net, but a click captured by DOM position tells you a button was pressed, not what it meant.

## Results

The site is live, the studio's positioning reads on it, and I carried the whole loop end to end: what we sell, to whom, the copy, the components, the deploy, the events. Nothing was lost in a handoff because there wasn't one.

The instrumentation has already paid for itself once, and in the direction that saves work: reading the baseline against Search Console showed the problem sat one step upstream, and stopped a conversion optimisation that had nothing to stand on. That is the most useful thing a site of your own can give you — not a chart proving a redesign worked, but the method that makes the next decision an informed one. Sometimes the informed decision is to leave it alone for now.
